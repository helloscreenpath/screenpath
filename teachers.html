For Teachers & AT Specialists

Bring ScreenPath into your classroom.

A short guide to what's here, how student progress is tracked, and how to get started.

What's on ScreenPath

ScreenPath is a fluency tool, not a teaching tool. It assumes a student already knows the basics, moving by heading, following links, reading a table, entering text, and gives them story-based and game-based ways to get faster and more confident using those skills. There are three kinds of activities.

The School — 3 rooms

An after-hours escape room. The School Office, The Library, and The Computer Lab, each built around one navigation skill.

Why it matters: jumping by heading and scanning a directory table under a little pressure is the same move as finding an assignment section in Canvas by heading, or reading down a grades table.

Headings · Links & Tables · Forms

The Doctor's Visit — 4 rooms

A school health office visit told across four rooms: check-in, exam room, vitals history, and the front desk.

Why it matters: editing pre-filled form fields mirrors updating a real registration or profile form. The auditory attention room builds the habit of catching information from audio with no script to follow along, useful for a lecture recording or an uncaptioned video. The two-table lookup and booking room mirrors reading a grade table or picking a time slot on a real scheduling page.

Edit Fields & Combo Boxes · Auditory Attention · Table Navigation · Interactive Tables & Booking

The Grocery Store: Dinner at Seven, 4 rooms

A Friday evening grocery run told across four rooms: sorting the list at the entrance, shopping the aisles, the express checkout, and getting out the door. Recommended for grades 6 to 12.

Why it matters: the first room uses the same dropdown format as a Canvas matching question, including answers that are used more than once and answers that are never used. The aisle tables pair table navigation with checkboxes and careful label reading, the checkout room builds the habit of verifying before acting and speaking up about a wrong price, and the last room contrasts radio buttons with checkboxes while practicing food safety, money, and community travel. Expanded Core Curriculum areas: compensatory skills, independent living skills, and self advocacy.

Matching Questions · Tables & Checkboxes · Links & Verification · Radio Buttons

Arcade — Word Guess

A quick, replayable spelling game with no ongoing story, good for a warm-up or a few spare minutes.

Why it matters: typing accurate guesses while acting on spoken feedback after every try builds the habit of trusting audio feedback instead of needing to see it confirmed, plus reinforces careful typing.

Spelling & Listening

Arcade — Are You Smarter Than a 5th Grader?

Climbs from 1st grade to 6th, answering questions across every subject, with lifelines that only work a limited number of times.

Why it matters: it's not just a fun game, it's quick navigation practice, jumping around the page to answer different question types under a little time pressure. That builds real confidence answering questions in a Canvas quiz or a Google Classroom assignment.

Recall · Assistive Technology Commands

Skill Drills — Writing Lab

Free Write for open-ended typing practice, or Dictation Practice to type back exactly what's read aloud.

Why it matters: Free Write builds general typing fluency for writing an actual assignment response. Dictation Practice builds listen-and-transcribe accuracy, the same skill used taking notes from something read aloud.

Typing Accuracy · Listening

The three escape room series offer Practice mode, no timer, for building confidence, and Challenge mode, timed, once a student is ready for pressure. Every activity now opens by asking which screen reader the student is using before anything else starts, so that context is captured for every session, not just the escape rooms.

How student progress is tracked

There are no accounts and nothing to set up. As a student works, each finished room, each round of a game, each Writing Lab session saves a record directly in their own browser. Nothing is sent anywhere automatically.

That history is visible anytime on the Progress page: rooms completed, attempts, time used, accuracy, and the dates a student worked on each activity.

One limitation worth knowing. Progress lives only in the browser a student actually played in. It does not follow them to a different computer, and clearing browser data clears it too. Because of that, the Progress page is most useful reviewed on the student's own device, ideally sitting with them, rather than checked remotely.

Right now, Writing Lab is the only activity with a built-in way for a student to email their results straight to you, or copy them to the clipboard, at the end of a session. The escape rooms and the Arcade games don't have that yet, so their history is best reviewed together on the Progress page.

Getting started

  1. No install, no login. Send a student the link and they can start right away, on any device with a browser.
  2. Start with The School, in Practice mode. Its three rooms cover headings, links and tables, and forms, the fundamentals the rest of the platform builds on.
  3. Let Practice mode do its job. There's no timer in Practice at all. Challenge mode is there once a student wants to add real time pressure.
  4. Check in on the Progress page together. It's designed to be read with a student, not just about them.
